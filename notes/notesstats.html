Measures of Central Tendency

A measure of central tendency summarizes a numerical dataset by a single value. Different measures capture different aspects of the data, and the word average is not precise until we specify which one we mean. We begin with arithmetic, weighted, geometric, and harmonic means. We will examine the median, quantiles, and mode next.

Definition (arithmetic mean).

For observations \(x_1,\ldots,x_n\) with \(n\geq 1\), the sample arithmetic mean is \[ \bar{x}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n x_i. \] The symbol \(\mu\) usually denotes a population mean (or the expectation of a random variable, when it exists), while \(\bar{x}\) denotes the mean computed from a sample. The sample mean is often used to estimate the unknown population mean, but it is itself a statistic: another random sample will generally give a different \(\bar{x}\).

Lemma (decomposition of squared deviations).

For real numbers \(x_1,\ldots,x_n\) with sample mean \(\bar{x}\), and every \(c\in\mathbb{R}\),

\[\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i-c)^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i-\bar{x})^2+n(c-\bar{x})^2.\]

Proof. Write \(x_i-c=(x_i-\bar{x})+(\bar{x}-c)\), square, and sum. The cross term vanishes because \(\sum_i(x_i-\bar{x})=0\).

Theorem (least-squares characterization of the mean).

The sample mean is the unique minimizer of the sum of squared deviations:

\[\bar{x}=\underset{c\in\mathbb{R}}{\operatorname{argmin}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i-c)^2.\]

Proof. By the lemma, the squared-deviation sum is a constant plus \(n(c-\bar{x})^2\). Since \(n\geq1\), the additional term is zero exactly when \(c=\bar{x}\).

This least-squares property is why arithmetic means appear naturally in squared-error loss and least-squares estimation. However, an extreme observation can shift the mean substantially.

Definition (weighted arithmetic mean).

If observations \(x_1,\ldots,x_n\) have nonnegative weights \(w_1,\ldots,w_n\), not all zero, the weighted arithmetic mean is \[ \bar{x}_w=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^n w_i x_i}{\sum_{i=1}^n w_i}. \]

For example, if a college doubles the weight of a mathematics score relative to English, a student scoring 70 in mathematics and 55 in English has a weighted score of \((2\cdot70+55)/3=65\). An equally weighted average would be 62.5.

Weighting is particularly important when combining summaries of groups of different sizes. If one group has 10 individuals with mean 80 and another has 90 individuals with mean 60, the combined mean is \((10\cdot80+90\cdot60)/100=62\), not 70. This is a common data-aggregation pitfall: the overall mean is not generally the simple mean of subgroup means. Here the weights are subgroup sizes, but other weighting schemes (for example, survey weights) have different interpretations.

Experiment 2: averaging subgroup means correctly.

import numpy as np

group_sizes = np.array([10, 90])
group_means = np.array([80.0, 60.0])

print(f"Unweighted mean of group means: {naive_mean:.1f}")
print(f"Mean over all individuals:    {overall_mean:.1f}")
assert np.isclose(overall_mean, 62.0)

Data science application: aggregating ratios and KPIs.

Dashboard metrics such as conversion rates, defect rates, click-through rates, and average revenue per transaction are ratios. To combine them over groups, recompute the ratio from its total numerator and total denominator. Taking the unweighted mean of group rates generally gives the wrong overall metric. For example, the overall conversion rate is \[ \frac{\text{total conversions}}{\text{total sessions}}, \] not generally the mean of daily or channel-specific conversion rates. This is a useful distinction when interpreting pandas.groupby output or aggregating SQL tables.

Experiment 3: conversion rates in a Pandas DataFrame.

import pandas as pd

Definition (geometric mean).

For strictly positive \(a_1,\ldots,a_n\), the geometric mean is \[ G=\left(\prod_{i=1}^n a_i\right)^{1/n}. \]

It is appropriate when equal-length periods contribute multiplicative factors. Suppose a population grows by 2% in the first year and by 5% in the second. Starting with one million people, the final population is \(1{,}000{,}000\cdot1.02\cdot1.05=1{,}071{,}000\). The arithmetic average of the two annual percentage changes is indeed 3.5%, but applying 3.5% in each year gives the slightly different result 1,071,225.

The equivalent constant annual compound rate is obtained by taking the geometric mean of the growth factors, not of the percentage changes themselves: \[ r_{\mathrm{compound}}= \left(\prod_{i=1}^n(1+r_i)\right)^{1/n}-1. \] In our example, \(\sqrt{1.02\cdot1.05}-1\approx0.0348913\), or about 3.4891% per year. The rates \(r_i\) can be negative, but this calculation assumes each growth factor \(1+r_i\) is positive; a -100% return is a special case and can never be recovered by subsequent ordinary percentage growth.

Experiment 4: the annualized compound growth rate.

from math import prod
from scipy.stats import gmean

print(f"Equivalent annual growth: {100 * equivalent_rate:.4f}%")

Definition (harmonic mean).

For positive values \(a_1,\ldots,a_n\), the harmonic mean is \[ H=\frac{n}{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^n 1/a_i}. \]

If two legs of a trip each cover 100 km, with speeds 50 km/h and 40 km/h, the average speed over the entire journey is total distance divided by total time: \[ \frac{200}{100/50+100/40}=\frac{2}{1/50+1/40} \approx44.44\ \text{km/h}. \] The arithmetic mean of the speeds (45 km/h) is not the journey's average speed. The harmonic mean is appropriate here because distances are equal, whereas the amount of time spent at each speed is not.

If the distances differ, use the distance-weighted harmonic mean: \[ H_w=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^n d_i}{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^n d_i/v_i}, \] where \(d_i\) and \(v_i\) are the distance and speed of segment \(i\). The same structure appears when aggregating densities. If city A has 1,000 people and density 50 people/km\(^2\), and city B has 3,000 people and density 40 people/km\(^2\), the overall density is \((1000+3000)/(1000/50+3000/40)\approx42.1053\) people/km\(^2\). This is the population-weighted harmonic mean of the cities' densities.

Experiment 5: equal distances and population density.

from scipy.stats import hmean

# Equal distances: the harmonic mean of the speeds is appropriate.
speeds = [50.0, 40.0]  # km/h
distance_each_way = 100.0  # km
direct_average_speed = (2 * distance_each_way) / sum(
    distance_each_way / speed for speed in speeds
)
print(f"Overall average speed: {direct_average_speed:.4f} km/h")
print(f"Harmonic mean of speeds: {hmean(speeds):.4f} km/h")

# Total population / total area gives the combined density.
densities = [50.0, 40.0]    # people/km^2
populations = [1000, 3000]  # people
areas = [pop / density for pop, density in zip(populations, densities)]
combined_density = sum(populations) / sum(areas)
weighted_harmonic = hmean(densities, weights=populations)
print(f"Combined density: {combined_density:.4f} people/km^2")
print(f"Weighted harmonic mean: {weighted_harmonic:.4f} people/km^2")

Definition (quadratic mean).

For real numbers \(a_1,\ldots,a_n\), the quadratic mean, or root mean square (RMS), is

\[\mathrm{QM}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i^2}.\]

Theorem (QM–AM–GM–HM inequalities).

For strictly positive \(a_1,\ldots,a_n\), the quadratic mean (root mean square), arithmetic mean, geometric mean, and harmonic mean satisfy \[ \underbrace{\sqrt{\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n a_i^2}}_{\mathrm{QM}} \;\geq\; \underbrace{\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n a_i}_{\mathrm{AM}} \;\geq\; \underbrace{\left(\prod_{i=1}^n a_i\right)^{1/n}}_{\mathrm{GM}} \;\geq\; \underbrace{\frac{n}{\sum_{i=1}^n 1/a_i}}_{\mathrm{HM}}. \] Equality throughout occurs if and only if all the values are equal. The quadratic mean, often called RMS, also appears in the calculation of root mean squared error.

Proof (optional). By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, \((\sum_i a_i)^2\leq n\sum_i a_i^2\), which gives \(\mathrm{QM}\geq\mathrm{AM}\). Since \(\log\) is concave, Jensen's inequality gives \(\frac1n\sum_i\log a_i\leq\log(\frac1n\sum_i a_i)\), so \(\mathrm{GM}\leq\mathrm{AM}\). Applying AM–GM to the positive reciprocals \(1/a_i\) yields \(\mathrm{HM}\leq\mathrm{GM}\). Equality throughout holds precisely when all values are equal.

Experiment 6: compare the four means.

import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import gmean, hmean

values = np.array([2.0, 4.0, 8.0])
quadratic = np.sqrt(np.mean(values ** 2))

harmonic = hmean(values)

print(f"QM = {quadratic:.4f}")
print(f"AM = {arithmetic:.4f}")
print(f"GM = {geometric:.4f}")

Practical considerations when working with data.

Before computing an average, check the units, missing observations, weights, and whether the quantity is additive or multiplicative. In NumPy, np.mean ordinarily propagates a missing value encoded as np.nan. The function np.nanmean ignores these values, but it does not address why the data are missing: excluding observations can bias an estimate if missingness is systematic.

Definition (sample median).

Let \(x_1,\ldots,x_n\) be real-valued observations. A median is a number \(m\in\mathbb{R}\) such that at least half the observations are less than or equal to \(m\), and at least half are greater than or equal to \(m\).

Equivalently, if \(x_{(1)}\leq\cdots\leq x_{(n)}\) denote the ordered observations, we commonly define the sample median as \[ \widetilde{x}= \begin{cases} x_{((n+1)/2)}, & n \text{ odd},\\[4pt] \dfrac{x_{(n/2)}+x_{(n/2+1)}}{2}, & n \text{ even}. \end{cases} \]

When \(n\) is odd, the median is the middle observation. When \(n\) is even, every number between the two middle observations satisfies the defining inequalities. Taking their arithmetic mean is the usual convention for obtaining a unique numerical summary.

Like the mean, the median measures central location, but the two summaries behave differently when the distribution is skewed or contains extreme observations.

Theorem (least-absolute-deviations characterization of the median).

Let \(x_1,\ldots,x_n\in\mathbb{R}\). A number \(m\in\mathbb{R}\) is a median of the observations if and only if it minimizes their total absolute deviation: \[ m\in\underset{c\in\mathbb{R}} {\operatorname{argmin}} \sum_{i=1}^{n}|x_i-c|. \]

In particular, when \(n\) is even, the set of minimizers is \[ [x_{(n/2)},x_{(n/2+1)}], \] whereas for odd \(n\), the minimizer is the middle observation.

Proof. Arrange the observations in increasing order and pair the smallest with the largest, the second smallest with the second largest, and so on. For \(a\leq b\), the expression \(|a-c|+|b-c|\) is minimized precisely when \(c\in[a,b]\). The intersection of the minimizing intervals of all pairs is the interval between the two middle observations. For odd \(n\), the remaining middle observation contributes an additional term, which is minimized at that observation.

Consequently, the mean and median solve two different optimization problems: \[ \begin{aligned} \bar{x} &=\underset{c\in\mathbb{R}} {\operatorname{argmin}} \sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i-c)^2,\\[6pt] \widetilde{x} &\in\underset{c\in\mathbb{R}} {\operatorname{argmin}} \sum_{i=1}^{n}|x_i-c|. \end{aligned} \] The first uses squared-error loss, while the second uses absolute-error loss. These are commonly called L2 and L1 loss, respectively.

Robustness of the median.

A statistic is called robust when its value is relatively insensitive to certain departures from the assumed data distribution, such as a small number of unusually large or small observations.

The arithmetic mean can be made arbitrarily large by increasing a single observation while keeping all other observations fixed. The median is considerably less sensitive to the magnitude of such an extreme observation. This does not mean that the median is completely unaffected by changes to the data.

For example, consider the observations \(1,2,3,4,5\). Their mean and median are both 3. Replacing the final observation by 1000 changes the mean to 202, while the median remains 3.

This distinction is particularly useful for skewed variables such as income, house prices, transaction values, and waiting times. In these settings, the median often better represents a typical observation, while the mean remains important when totals or expected values are of interest.

The median divides an ordered dataset into two halves. More generally, quantiles describe the distribution of observations by identifying values associated with specified proportions of the data.

For example, the 90th percentile of a response-time distribution provides a threshold associated with the fastest 90% of observations. Quantiles are widely used to summarize income distributions, transaction values, customer behavior, and system performance.

Definition (empirical quantile).

Let \(D=(x_1,\ldots,x_n)\) be a dataset of real numbers and let \(p\in(0,1)\). A real number \(y\) is an empirical \(p\)-quantile if \[ \frac{\#\{i:x_i<y\}}{n}\leq p \quad\text{and}\quad \frac{\#\{i:x_i>y\}}{n}\leq1-p. \]

Thus, at most \(100p\%\) of the observations lie strictly below \(y\), and at most \(100(1-p)\%\) lie strictly above it.

Such a quantile always exists, but it need not be unique. For example, consider the ordered dataset \[ D=(10,20,30,40). \] Every number in the interval \([10,20]\) satisfies the defining inequalities for \(p=0.25\). Similarly, every number in \([20,30]\) is a median according to the general quantile definition.

At the endpoints \(p=0\) and \(p=1\), it is customary to select the minimum and maximum of the dataset, respectively.

Connection with the empirical distribution function.

Definition (empirical cumulative distribution function).

For observations \(x_1,\ldots,x_n\), the empirical cumulative distribution function (ECDF) is \[ F_n(x)=\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}_{\{x_i\leq x\}}, \qquad x\in\mathbb{R}, \] where \(\mathbf{1}_{\{x_i\leq x\}}\) equals 1 when \(x_i\leq x\) and 0 otherwise.

The value \(F_n(x)\) is the proportion of observations less than or equal to \(x\). It is a nondecreasing, right-continuous step function, with jumps at observed values. Tied observations produce larger jumps.

We can use the ECDF to select one particular empirical quantile, even when the defining inequalities allow several values.

Definition (inverse empirical CDF).

For \(p\in(0,1]\), define \[ Q_n(p)=\min\{x\in\mathbb{R}:F_n(x)\geq p\}. \] This is the smallest value at which the ECDF reaches or exceeds \(p\).

Lemma (empirical quantile as an order statistic).

Let \(x_{(1)}\leq\cdots\leq x_{(n)}\) denote the ordered observations. Then \[ Q_n(p)=x_{(\lceil np\rceil)}, \qquad 0<p\leq1, \] where \(\lceil t\rceil\) denotes the smallest integer greater than or equal to \(t\).

Proof. The ECDF reaches or exceeds \(p\) precisely when at least \(\lceil np\rceil\) observations have been included. The smallest value for which this happens is the corresponding order statistic.

For the dataset \(D=(10,20,30,40)\), this definition gives \[ Q_n(0.25)=10,\qquad Q_n(0.50)=20,\qquad Q_n(0.75)=30. \] In NumPy, this convention corresponds to method="inverted_cdf".

Interpolated quantiles.

Statistical software often uses interpolation to estimate quantiles between observed data values. There are several such conventions, and their results need not agree.

NumPy's default method, called linear, uses the position \[ h=(n-1)p \] in the zero-indexed ordered data. If \(h=j+\gamma\), where \(j=\lfloor h\rfloor\) and \(0<\gamma<1\), the estimate is \[ Q_{\mathrm{linear}}(p) =(1-\gamma)x_{(j+1)} +\gamma x_{(j+2)}. \] If \(h\) is an integer, the corresponding observation is used directly.

For \(D=(10,20,30,40)\) and \(p=0.25\), \[ h=3(0.25)=0.75, \] and therefore \[ Q_{\mathrm{linear}}(0.25) =0.25(10)+0.75(20)=17.5. \] By contrast, the inverse-ECDF convention gives 10.

The interpolated method is a sample-based estimate of a population quantile. Unlike the inverse ECDF, it does not necessarily return an observed data value. For some probability levels, the interpolated result may not even satisfy the exact empirical coverage inequalities stated earlier. These are different conventions, not contradictory calculations.

Definition (percentile).

The \(k\)th percentile, for \(0\leq k\leq100\), is a quantile associated with probability level \(p=k/100\), under the chosen quantile convention.

Thus, the 90th percentile corresponds to the \(0.90\)-quantile, and the 99th percentile to the \(0.99\)-quantile. In NumPy, np.quantile expects probabilities between 0 and 1, whereas np.percentile expects percentages between 0 and 100.

Definition (quartiles).

Quartiles are quantiles dividing an ordered dataset into four parts: \[ Q_1=Q(0.25),\qquad Q_2=Q(0.50),\qquad Q_3=Q(0.75). \] The second quartile is a median. The precise numerical values depend on the quantile convention used.

Percentiles describe thresholds, not probabilities assigned to individual observations. Saying that a response time is at the 95th percentile does not mean that this particular response has a probability of 95%.

For a continuous population distribution, the 95th percentile marks a threshold below which 95% of probability lies. In a finite sample, ties and quantile conventions can prevent exactly 95% of observations from lying below the reported threshold.

Application: monitoring response times.

A system's mean response time may look satisfactory even when some users experience substantial delays. For this reason, performance monitoring often reports the median and high percentiles, such as p95 or p99.

These summaries answer different questions. The median describes a typical response, whereas p95 and p99 describe the upper tail. They should be interpreted together with the sample size and observation period.

Sampling variability of estimated quantiles.

An empirical quantile is calculated from a sample and is therefore subject to sampling variability. Different random samples from the same population generally give different percentile estimates.

Definition (sample mode).

Let \(D=(x_1,\ldots,x_n)\) be a dataset, and let \(n(a)=\#\{i:x_i=a\}\) denote the frequency of a value \(a\). A mode is any observed value \(m\) satisfying \[ n(m)=\max_a n(a). \]

The mode identifies the most frequently occurring value in a dataset. Unlike the mean and median, it is also meaningful for categorical data. For example, we can determine the most common payment method, product category, or customer preference.

A dataset can have more than one mode. If exactly two values share the highest frequency, the dataset is called bimodal under the frequency definition. More generally, several values may share the maximum frequency.

If all observations occur equally often, every distinct observed value satisfies the mathematical definition. In particular, when all observations are unique, the sample mode provides little useful information about central tendency. Some descriptions call such datasets modeless by convention.

The sample mode should be distinguished from the mode of an underlying probability distribution. Definition (five-number summary).

Definition (interquartile range).

The interquartile range (IQR) is defined by \[ \mathrm{IQR}=Q_3-Q_1. \] It measures the width of the interval between the first and third quartiles, which represents the central half of a distribution in the quantile sense.

The IQR is a measure of dispersion, rather than central location. Two datasets can have the same median but very different IQRs.

Compared with the range and standard deviation, the IQR is relatively insensitive to the magnitudes of a small number of extreme observations. This makes it useful for skewed distributions and data containing unusually large or small values.

Property (translation and scaling of the IQR).

Suppose the quantiles are computed using the linear interpolation convention. If every observation is transformed according to \(y_i=ax_i+b\), where \(a>0\), then \[ \mathrm{IQR}(y_1,\ldots,y_n) =a\,\mathrm{IQR}(x_1,\ldots,x_n). \]

Proof. A strictly increasing affine transformation preserves the ordering of observations. The linear quantile rule therefore gives \(Q_1(Y)=aQ_1(X)+b\) and \(Q_3(Y)=aQ_3(X)+b\). Subtracting eliminates \(b\) and gives the result.

Thus, adding a constant to every observation leaves the IQR unchanged. Multiplying all observations by a positive constant multiplies the IQR by the same constant. This is useful when converting measurement units or transforming numerical features.

Box plots.

A box plot visualizes numerical data using the median, quartiles, whiskers, and possible outliers. It is particularly useful for comparing distributions across groups.

In a standard Tukey-style box plot, the box extends from \(Q_1\) to \(Q_3\), and a line inside the box marks the median. The length of the box is the IQR. The whiskers extend toward the most extreme observations that are not classified as outliers by the chosen whisker rule.

Importantly, the whiskers do not necessarily extend to the minimum and maximum. Some alternative box-plot conventions do use the minimum and maximum, so the plotting convention should be stated when necessary.

Definition (Tukey's 1.5-IQR fences).

Given quartiles \(Q_1\) and \(Q_3\), define the lower and upper fences by \[ L=Q_1-1.5\,\mathrm{IQR}, \qquad U=Q_3+1.5\,\mathrm{IQR}. \] An observation is flagged as a potential outlier when it is strictly smaller than \(L\) or strictly greater than \(U\).

In a Tukey-style box plot, the lower whisker reaches the smallest observed value greater than or equal to \(L\), and the upper whisker reaches the largest observed value less than or equal to \(U\). Observations outside these limits are displayed individually.

The fences are calculated thresholds, not necessarily observed data values. They should not be confused with the actual whisker endpoints.

Interpreting outliers carefully.

Tukey's 1.5-IQR rule is a useful exploratory heuristic, but a flagged observation is not automatically an error. It may be a valid measurement from the tail of a distribution, an unusual but important event, or evidence that the data contain different subpopulations.

The rule has several limitations. Its behavior depends on the distribution's shape, the sample size, and the convention used to calculate quartiles. In strongly skewed distributions, many valid observations may lie beyond the upper fence. With highly discrete data or many repeated values, the IQR can even be zero.

Moreover, a standard box plot does not distinguish between errors, rare observations, and genuine extreme events. Outlier removal should therefore be justified by the data-generating process and the objective of the analysis, rather than applied automatically.

Even observations generated from a normal distribution can fall outside the 1.5-IQR fences. For a standard normal distribution, the population quartiles are approximately \(-0.6745\) and \(0.6745\). Consequently, the corresponding population fences are approximately \(-2.698\) and \(2.698\). There is a small but positive probability of observing values outside these limits.

An additional robust measure of spread.

The IQR measures dispersion using the first and third quartiles. Another useful robust measure is the median absolute deviation, or MAD. It measures the typical absolute distance from the sample median.

Definition (median absolute deviation).

For observations \(x_1,\ldots,x_n\) with sample median \(\widetilde{x}\), the median absolute deviation is \[ \mathrm{MAD} =\operatorname{median} \left( |x_1-\widetilde{x}|,\ldots, |x_n-\widetilde{x}| \right). \]

Like the IQR, the MAD is relatively insensitive to the magnitudes of a small number of extreme observations. It is often useful when a robust measure of variability is required.

For a normal population, multiplying the raw MAD by approximately \(1.4826\) produces a scale measure that is consistent for the population standard deviation. This adjustment is specific to the normal reference distribution and should not be interpreted as a universal identity between MAD and standard deviation.

A single numerical summary rarely describes a distribution adequately. For approximately symmetric data without substantial outliers, the mean and standard deviation are often informative. Distribution and Quantile Functions

The distribution of a random variable describes how probability is assigned to its possible values. Two closely related functions are particularly useful for describing distributions: the cumulative distribution function (CDF) and the quantile function.

The CDF answers questions of the form What is the probability that a random variable does not exceed a given value? The quantile function addresses the reverse question: What threshold corresponds to a specified cumulative probability?

Unlike the empirical quantiles discussed earlier, we now consider the distribution of a random variable itself. The mathematical definitions apply to discrete, continuous, and mixed distributions.

Definition (cumulative distribution function).

Let \(X\) be a real-valued random variable. Its cumulative distribution function (CDF) is the function \(F:\mathbb{R}\to[0,1]\) defined by \[ F(x)=\mathbb{P}(X\leq x), \qquad x\in\mathbb{R}. \]

Thus, \(F(x)\) is the probability that \(X\) takes a value less than or equal to \(x\). The CDF is defined for every real-valued random variable, regardless of whether its distribution has a probability density function.

Theorem (basic properties of a CDF).

Every cumulative distribution function \(F\) satisfies the following properties:

  1. Monotonicity: If \(x_1\leq x_2\), then \(F(x_1)\leq F(x_2)\).
  2. Limits: \[ \lim_{x\to-\infty}F(x)=0, \qquad \lim_{x\to\infty}F(x)=1. \]
  3. Right-continuity: For every \(x\in\mathbb{R}\), \[ \lim_{h\downarrow0}F(x+h)=F(x). \]

Proof. Monotonicity follows because \(\{X\leq x_1\}\subseteq\{X\leq x_2\}\) whenever \(x_1\leq x_2\). The limits follow from the continuity of probability for increasing and decreasing sequences of events. For right-continuity, let \(x_n\downarrow x\). The events \(\{X\leq x_n\}\) decrease to \(\{X\leq x\}\), so their probabilities converge to \(F(x)\).

Conversely, every real-valued function satisfying these three properties is the CDF of some probability distribution on \(\mathbb{R}\). Thus, these properties characterize cumulative distribution functions.

Calculating probabilities from a CDF.

The CDF determines the probability of every interval. For example, if \(a<b\), then \[ \mathbb{P}(a<X\leq b)=F(b)-F(a). \] This follows by splitting the event \(\{X\leq b\}\) into the disjoint events \(\{X\leq a\}\) and \(\{a<X\leq b\}\).

To handle strict and non-strict inequalities correctly, we introduce the left limit \[ F(x^-)=\lim_{t\uparrow x}F(t) =\mathbb{P}(X<x). \]

We can then express several useful probabilities: \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{P}(X>x)&=1-F(x),\\ \mathbb{P}(X\geq x)&=1-F(x^-),\\ \mathbb{P}(a\leq X\leq b)&=F(b)-F(a^-),\\ \mathbb{P}(X=x)&=F(x)-F(x^-). \end{aligned} \]

The final identity is particularly important. It states that the probability assigned to an individual value equals the size of the jump in the CDF at that value. Consequently, a CDF can have discontinuities precisely at values carrying positive probability.

Distributions with probability density functions.

Suppose \(X\) has a probability density function \(f:\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty)\). Then \[ F(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}f(t)\,dt. \] At points where \(f\) is continuous, we also have \[ F'(x)=f(x). \]

The density \(f(x)\) is not the probability that \(X=x\). Rather, probabilities are obtained by integrating the density over intervals: \[ \mathbb{P}(a\leq X\leq b) =\int_a^b f(t)\,dt. \]

Since a single point has zero width, a random variable with a density satisfies \[ \mathbb{P}(X=x)=0 \] for every \(x\in\mathbb{R}\). Therefore, the choice between strict and non-strict inequalities does not affect interval probabilities in this case.

A continuous CDF does not necessarily possess a density. The integral representation above applies to distributions that are absolutely continuous. This distinction is mathematically important, even though most standard continuous distributions used in introductory statistics have densities.

The CDF of a discrete distribution.

If \(X\) is a discrete random variable with probability mass function \(p_X(x)=\mathbb{P}(X=x)\), then \[ F(x)=\sum_{t\leq x}p_X(t), \] where the sum is over the possible values of \(X\).

The CDF of a discrete distribution is a step function. It remains constant between possible values of the random variable and jumps at values with positive probability. The height of the jump at \(x\) is exactly \(p_X(x)\).

For example, suppose that \[ X\sim\operatorname{Binomial}(5,0.3). \] Then \(X\) represents the number of successes in five independent Bernoulli trials, each having success probability \(0.3\). The CDF is \[ F(k)=\sum_{j=0}^{k} \binom{5}{j}(0.3)^j(0.7)^{5-j}, \] for integers \(k\) between 0 and 5.

From cumulative probabilities to quantiles.

We now turn to the inverse problem. Given a probability level \(p\), we want to determine a value \(x\) at which the CDF reaches that level.

If \(F\) is continuous and strictly increasing over the support of its distribution, the equation \(F(x)=p\) has a unique solution for every \(p\in(0,1)\). However, an arbitrary CDF may have jumps or intervals on which it is constant. Therefore, it need not possess an ordinary inverse.

Definition (quantile function).

Let \(X\) be a real-valued random variable with CDF \(F\). Its quantile function \(Q:(0,1)\to\mathbb{R}\) is defined by \[ Q(p)=\inf\{x\in\mathbb{R}:F(x)\geq p\}, \qquad 0<p<1. \]

Since a CDF is nondecreasing and right-continuous, the infimum is attained for every \(p\in(0,1)\). Equivalently, we may write \[ Q(p)=\min\{x\in\mathbb{R}:F(x)\geq p\}. \] This function is also known as the generalized inverse of \(F\). It is commonly denoted by \(F^{-1}\), although this notation does not necessarily represent an ordinary inverse function.

We restrict the definition to \(p\in(0,1)\). Unlike empirical distributions from finite datasets, an arbitrary population distribution need not have finite endpoints. For example, a normal distribution has no finite 0th or 100th percentile.

Theorem (properties of the quantile function).

Let \(F\) be a CDF and \(Q\) its generalized quantile function. Then:

  1. \(Q\) is nondecreasing.
  2. For every \(p\in(0,1)\), \[ F(Q(p)^-)\leq p\leq F(Q(p)). \]
  3. If \(F\) is continuous, then \[ F(Q(p))=p. \]

Proof. If \(p_1\leq p_2\), then \(\{x:F(x)\geq p_2\}\) is contained in \(\{x:F(x)\geq p_1\}\). Consequently, \(Q(p_1)\leq Q(p_2)\). By the definition of \(Q(p)\), every value strictly smaller than \(Q(p)\) has CDF value smaller than \(p\), while right-continuity gives \(F(Q(p))\geq p\). Taking the left limit yields the second assertion. If \(F\) is continuous, its left limit equals its value at \(Q(p)\), giving equality.

The distinction between continuous and discrete distributions is important. For a continuous CDF, the cumulative probability at the selected quantile is exactly \(p\). For a discrete distribution, the CDF may jump over \(p\), giving \(F(Q(p))>p\).

The generalized quantile function selects one specific quantile. More generally, a number \(x\) satisfying \[ \mathbb{P}(X<x)\leq p \quad\text{and}\quad \mathbb{P}(X\leq x)\geq p \] can be called a quantile of order \(p\). Such a number need not be unique. The generalized inverse selects the smallest one.

For example, let \(X\) be uniformly distributed over the integers \(1,2,3,4\). Every value in \([2,3]\) is a median under the general definition, but the generalized quantile function selects \(Q(0.5)=2\). This also explains why different conventions for medians can produce different numerical values.

Distributions with discrete and continuous components.

Not every distribution is purely discrete or absolutely continuous. Some probability models combine point masses with a continuous component. Such distributions are particularly relevant when a variable can take a special value, often zero, with positive probability.

For example, suppose a random variable \(X\) equals zero with probability \(0.25\). With probability \(0.75\), it follows an exponential distribution with rate 1. Then \[ F(x)= \begin{cases} 0, & x<0,\\ 1-0.75e^{-x}, & x\geq0. \end{cases} \]

The CDF jumps from 0 to 0.25 at zero and then increases continuously toward 1. Its quantile function is \[ Q(p)= \begin{cases} 0, & 0<p\leq0.25,\\ -\log\left(\dfrac{1-p}{0.75}\right), & 0.25<p<1. \end{cases} \]

This is an example of a zero-inflated distribution. Similar models can be useful for nonnegative variables with many zeros, such as purchase amounts, waiting times, or usage measurements.

Generating random variables from quantile functions.

Quantile functions are not only useful for reporting percentiles. They also provide a general method for simulating random variables from known distributions.

Theorem (inverse transform sampling).

Let \(F\) be a CDF with generalized quantile function \(Q\). If \[ U\sim\operatorname{Uniform}(0,1), \] then the random variable \[ X=Q(U) \] has CDF \(F\).

Proof. By the definition of the generalized inverse, for \(u\in(0,1)\), \[ Q(u)\leq x \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad u\leq F(x). \] Therefore, \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{P}(Q(U)\leq x) &=\mathbb{P}(U\leq F(x))\\ &=F(x), \end{aligned} \] since \(U\) is uniformly distributed on \((0,1)\). Hence \(Q(U)\) has the desired distribution.

The theorem holds for discrete, continuous, and mixed distributions. It is an important theoretical basis for random-variable simulation.

There is also a related result in the opposite direction. If \(X\) has a continuous CDF \(F\), then \(F(X)\) has the uniform distribution on \((0,1)\). This is known as the probability integral transform. The ordinary result does not generally hold when \(F\) has jumps.

As an example, consider an exponentially distributed random variable with rate \(\lambda>0\). Its CDF is \[ F(x)=1-e^{-\lambda x}, \qquad x\geq0. \] Solving \(F(x)=p\) gives \[ Q(p)=-\frac{\log(1-p)}{\lambda}. \] Consequently, transforming a uniform random variable using this formula produces an exponentially distributed random variable.

Hypotheses

Statistical hypothesis testing is a method of statistical inference used to assess claims about an unknown population parameter or data-generating process. Based on observed data, we decide whether there is sufficient evidence to reject a specified null hypothesis in favor of an alternative hypothesis.

Hypothesis testing does not establish whether a claim is true with certainty. Because observations are random, different samples may lead to different conclusions, even when the underlying population remains unchanged.

From a research question to a statistical parameter.

Before formulating hypotheses, we must identify the population of interest and the unknown quantity about which we want to draw conclusions. Such a quantity is called a parameter.

Common examples include the population mean \(\mu\), population proportion \(p\), population variance \(\sigma^2\), the difference between two population means \(\mu_1-\mu_2\), and the difference between two population proportions \(p_1-p_2\).

The distinction between a parameter and a statistic is essential. A parameter describes a population or its probability distribution, whereas a statistic is computed from observed data. For example, the sample mean \(\bar{X}\) is a statistic used to estimate the population mean \(\mu\).

In the frequentist framework considered here, the unknown parameter is treated as fixed, while the observations and the statistics computed from them are random before the data are collected.

Definition (statistical model).

A statistical model is a family of probability distributions \[ \mathcal{P}= \{P_\theta:\theta\in\Theta\}, \] where \(\Theta\) is the parameter space and \(\theta\) represents the unknown parameter, possibly consisting of several components.

The model describes the distributions considered possible for the observations. The assumptions defining this family are separate from the hypotheses about the unknown parameter.

For example, we may assume that observations are independent and normally distributed with mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2\). We might then formulate a hypothesis about \(\mu\). The hypothesis concerns the mean; the normality and independence assumptions describe the model used to investigate it.

Definition (null and alternative hypotheses).

Let \(\Theta_0\) and \(\Theta_1\) be disjoint subsets of the parameter space \(\Theta\), with \(\Theta_0\cup\Theta_1=\Theta\). We define the null hypothesis and alternative hypothesis by \[ H_0:\theta\in\Theta_0, \qquad H_1:\theta\in\Theta_1. \]

The null hypothesis \(H_0\) represents the set of parameter values against which evidence is evaluated. The alternative \(H_1\) specifies the competing parameter values of interest.

A common null hypothesis states that a population parameter equals a specified reference value. For instance, if \(\mu_0\) denotes a proposed population mean, we may test \[ H_0:\mu=\mu_0 \qquad\text{against}\qquad H_1:\mu\neq\mu_0. \]

It is important to recognize that hypotheses concern the unknown population parameter, not the observed value of a sample statistic. We do not formulate a population-mean test as \(H_0:\bar{X}=\mu_0\), because \(\bar{X}\) is a random statistic rather than the parameter being investigated.

Definition (one-sided and two-sided hypotheses).

For a real-valued parameter \(\theta\) and reference value \(\theta_0\), three common hypothesis formulations are:

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Two-sided:}\quad &H_0:\theta=\theta_0, &H_1:\theta\neq\theta_0,\\[4pt] \text{Right-sided:}\quad &H_0:\theta\leq\theta_0, &H_1:\theta>\theta_0,\\[4pt] \text{Left-sided:}\quad &H_0:\theta\geq\theta_0, &H_1:\theta<\theta_0. \end{aligned} \]

A two-sided test is appropriate when deviations in either direction are relevant. A right-sided test investigates whether the parameter exceeds a reference value, while a left-sided test investigates whether it falls below that value.

For example, suppose a factory fills packages with a target mean weight of \(500\,\mathrm{g}\). If both overfilling and underfilling are important, we might test \[ H_0:\mu=500 \qquad\text{against}\qquad H_1:\mu\neq500. \] If the only concern is underfilling, we might instead test \[ H_0:\mu\geq500 \qquad\text{against}\qquad H_1:\mu<500. \]

In introductory calculations, one-sided tests are often written using the boundary null \(H_0:\theta=\theta_0\). For many standard tests, this boundary determines the relevant reference distribution and critical value. Nevertheless, the full one-sided null generally contains a range of parameter values.

The direction of the alternative should be determined by the scientific question before inspecting the observed results. Choosing a one-sided alternative merely because the sample happened to move in that direction can invalidate the intended error control.

Definition (simple and composite hypotheses).

A hypothesis is called simple if it specifies a single probability distribution for the observations. Otherwise, it is called composite.

For example, suppose \[ X_1,\ldots,X_n \overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}N(\mu,\sigma^2). \] If \(\sigma^2\) is known, the hypothesis \(H_0:\mu=\mu_0\) specifies the complete sampling distribution and is therefore simple.

If \(\sigma^2\) is unknown, the same statement \(H_0:\mu=\mu_0\) permits many possible normal distributions with different variances. It is therefore composite.

Parameters that influence the sampling distribution but are not the principal objects of investigation are often called nuisance parameters. The unknown variance in the preceding example is a nuisance parameter when our primary interest concerns the population mean.

This distinction matters when selecting a test: the sampling distribution of a statistic may depend on unknown nuisance parameters, and different procedures handle them differently.

From observations to a test statistic.

Suppose we observe a random vector \(X=(X_1,\ldots,X_n)\), with realized values \(\mathbf{x}=(x_1,\ldots,x_n)\). A test statistic is a function of the observations, \[ T=T(X_1,\ldots,X_n), \] designed to measure some aspect of the evidence relevant to the hypotheses.

For a hypothesis concerning a population mean, the sample mean \[ \overline{X} =\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_i \] is a natural starting point. Its observed value \(\bar{x}\) measures the sample's average, but we must also account for sampling variability when assessing how unusual that value is under \(H_0\).

For example, suppose the observations are independent and normally distributed, with known standard deviation \(\sigma\). Under the equality null \(H_0:\mu=\mu_0\), the standardized statistic \[ Z= \frac{\overline{X}-\mu_0}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}} \] has a standard normal distribution: \[ Z\sim N(0,1)\qquad\text{under }H_0. \]

The numerator measures the difference between the sample mean and the hypothesized mean. The denominator is the standard deviation of the sample mean, commonly called its standard error. Thus, \(Z\) measures the observed deviation in units of standard errors.

The stated standard normal result is exact for independent normal observations with known \(\sigma\). Other tests may use approximations or reference distributions such as Student's t, chi-square, or F-distributions, depending on the model and the question.

Definition (rejection region).

Let \(S\) denote the sample space of the observed data. A statistical test specifies a subset \(R\subseteq S\), called its rejection region or critical region. We reject \(H_0\) if the observed outcome \(\mathbf{x}\) belongs to \(R\), and otherwise fail to reject \(H_0\).

A rejection region is frequently expressed through a test statistic. For instance, a right-sided test may reject when \[ T(\mathbf{x})>c, \] where \(c\) is a selected critical value. For a two-sided test using a signed statistic, rejection may occur when \[ |T(\mathbf{x})|>c. \]

The critical region must be chosen in a way that controls the probability of rejecting a true null hypothesis. We will study this principle in the following section on significance levels and error probabilities.

Statistical tests have two standard decision outcomes: reject \(H_0\) or fail to reject \(H_0\). Failing to reject does not establish that the null hypothesis is true. It means that the observed data do not provide sufficiently strong evidence against it under the chosen test and its assumptions.

Similarly, rejecting \(H_0\) does not demonstrate that every modeling assumption is correct or that the alternative explanation is scientifically adequate. A hypothesis test evaluates its specified statistical claim within a model.

Application: comparing conversion rates in an A/B test.

Hypothesis testing is widely used in data science to compare products, algorithms, interfaces, and business processes. An A/B test is a common example: users are randomly assigned to two alternatives, and we compare an outcome of interest.

Suppose version A is an existing checkout page and version B is a new design. Let \(p_A\) and \(p_B\) denote the probabilities of conversion for the corresponding user populations under the study design.

Hypotheses, assumptions, and interpretation.

A statistical hypothesis is not the same as a model assumption. For example, testing \(H_0:\mu=\mu_0\) using a normal-theory procedure does not automatically verify normality or independence.

Moreover, even a correctly calculated test cannot overcome serious problems with sampling or study design. A small p-value from a biased sample does not establish that the corresponding population conclusion is valid.

It is also important to distinguish statistical evidence from practical importance. A very small difference may be detectable with a sufficiently large sample, even when the difference is of little practical interest.

In contrast, a study with insufficient data may fail to detect a difference that would be important in practice. Estimating the effect size and its uncertainty can therefore be as important as testing a null hypothesis.

Finally, when the goal is to demonstrate that two treatments are sufficiently similar, an ordinary failure to reject a difference is not adequate evidence of equivalence. An equivalence test requires a prespecified margin describing what counts as a practically negligible difference.

Questions to answer before performing a test.

  • Population: Which population or data-generating process is the conclusion intended to describe?
  • Parameter: What unknown numerical quantity is being investigated?
  • Hypotheses: What are \(H_0\) and \(H_1\), and is the alternative one-sided or two-sided?
  • Study design: How were observations collected? Are they independent, paired, or clustered?
  • Model: Which distributional assumptions are needed for the chosen procedure?
  • Practical importance: What magnitude of difference would matter in the application?

Error and Significance Level

A statistical test makes a decision using random data. Even when the test is correctly designed, that decision can be wrong. We distinguish between rejecting a true null hypothesis and failing to reject a false null hypothesis, and we study how frequently these errors occur under repeated sampling. All probabilities below are conditional on a specified statistical model and data-generating process.

Definition (type I and type II errors).

A type I error occurs when a statistical test rejects a true null hypothesis. A type II error occurs when the test fails to reject a false null hypothesis.

Decision \(H_0\) is true \(H_0\) is false
Reject \(H_0\) Type I error (false positive) Correct rejection (true positive)
Fail to reject \(H_0\) Correct non-rejection Type II error (false negative)

For example, suppose a factory tests whether the mean filling weight of a machine differs from \(500\,\mathrm{g}\). We formulate \(H_0:\mu=500\) versus \(H_1:\mu\neq500\). Stopping a correctly calibrated machine because we reject \(H_0\) is a type I error. Failing to detect a genuinely miscalibrated machine is a type II error. The practical costs of these mistakes need not be equal.

Definition (power function).

Let \(X=(X_1,\ldots,X_n)\) denote the random observations, and let \(R\) be the rejection region of a test. For a statistical model \(\{P_\theta:\theta\in\Theta\}\), the power function is \[ \pi(\theta)=P_\theta(X\in R). \] Thus, \(\pi(\theta)\) is the probability of rejecting \(H_0\) when the true parameter is \(\theta\).

Let \(\Theta_0\) and \(\Theta_1\) denote the parameter values covered by the null and alternative hypotheses. The interpretation of the power function depends on which hypothesis is true: \[ \begin{aligned} \theta\in\Theta_0:\quad &\pi(\theta)=P_\theta(\text{type I error}),\\[4pt] \theta\in\Theta_1:\quad &\beta(\theta)=1-\pi(\theta) =P_\theta(\text{type II error}),\\[4pt] \theta\in\Theta_1:\quad &1-\beta(\theta)=\pi(\theta) =P_\theta(\text{correct rejection}). \end{aligned} \]

The quantity \(\beta(\theta)\) is the type II error probability at the specified alternative \(\theta\), and \(1-\beta(\theta)\) is the test's power there. For a composite alternative, power generally depends on the true parameter value. Consequently, saying only that a test has "80% power" is incomplete without specifying the effect of interest, the model, and the test design.

Definition (size and significance level).

The size of a test is its largest rejection probability under the null hypothesis: \[ \operatorname{size}(R) =\sup_{\theta\in\Theta_0}P_\theta(X\in R). \] A test has significance level \(\alpha\) if \[ \operatorname{size}(R)\leq\alpha. \]

Thus, a level-\(0.05\) test controls the probability of rejecting a true null to at most \(5\%\), for every parameter value in the null hypothesis, provided the model assumptions hold. The actual size may be smaller than the chosen nominal level, especially for discrete tests. A test whose size equals \(\alpha\) is called an exact-size \(\alpha\) test.

The significance level is a design property of the testing procedure, normally selected before examining the outcomes. It is not the probability that the null hypothesis is true and does not say that \(5\%\) of all statistically significant findings must be false.

Worked example: detecting a biased coin.

Toss a coin independently ten times, and let \(X\) denote the number of heads. Consider the one-sided hypotheses \[ H_0:p\leq0.5 \qquad\text{against}\qquad H_1:p>0.5, \] where \(p\) is the probability of heads. Under a fixed value of \(p\), \(X\sim\operatorname{Binomial}(10,p)\). Suppose our rejection rule is \(X\geq9\).

The rejection probability is largest under the null at its boundary \(p=0.5\). Therefore the size of this test is \[ \begin{aligned} \sup_{p\leq0.5}P_p(X\geq9) &=P_{0.5}(X\geq9)\\ &=\binom{10}{9}(0.5)^{10}+(0.5)^{10}\\ &=\frac{11}{1024}\approx0.01074. \end{aligned} \] This is a valid level-\(0.05\) test, although its actual size is only about \(1.07\%\). Rejecting at eight heads instead would give \[ P_{0.5}(X\geq8)=\frac{56}{1024}\approx0.05469, \] exceeding the proposed level. The discrete distribution prevents either of these non-randomized threshold rules from attaining size exactly \(0.05\).

Now suppose the coin is genuinely biased, with \(p=0.8\). The power is \[ \begin{aligned} \pi(0.8) &=P_{0.8}(X\geq9)\\ &=10(0.8)^9(0.2)+(0.8)^{10}\\ &\approx0.37581. \end{aligned} \] Hence \(\beta(0.8)\approx0.62419\). Even for this strongly biased coin, the test fails to detect the deviation in approximately \(62.4\%\) of repeated experiments.

This example illustrates the central trade-off: requiring more evidence before rejecting reduces false alarms but can also increase the chance of missing a genuine effect.

Worked example: power of a one-sided normal-mean test.

Suppose \(X_1,\ldots,X_n\) are independent observations from \(N(\mu,\sigma^2)\), with known \(\sigma=10\). We want to test \[ H_0:\mu\leq100 \qquad\text{against}\qquad H_1:\mu>100 \] at level \(\alpha=0.05\), using \(n=25\) observations. Since \[ \overline{X}\sim N\left(\mu,\frac{\sigma^2}{n}\right), \qquad \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}=2, \] the rejection rule is \[ \begin{aligned} Z=\frac{\overline{X}-100}{2} &>z_{0.95}\approx1.64485,\\ \text{equivalently}\quad\overline{X} &>c=100+2z_{0.95}\approx103.2897. \end{aligned} \] Here \(z_{0.95}=\Phi^{-1}(0.95)\), where \(\Phi\) is the standard normal CDF.

At the boundary \(\mu=100\), the rejection probability equals \(0.05\). For every \(\mu<100\), it is smaller. Therefore the test has size \(0.05\) over the entire composite null \(\mu\leq100\).

Theorem (power function of the one-sided z-test).

Under the stated independent normal model with known \(\sigma\), the test rejecting \(H_0:\mu\leq\mu_0\) when \[ \overline{X}>\mu_0+ z_{1-\alpha}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n} \] has power function \[ \pi(\mu)=1-\Phi\left( z_{1-\alpha} -\frac{\mu-\mu_0}{\sigma/\sqrt n} \right). \]

Proof. Let \(c=\mu_0+z_{1-\alpha}\sigma/\sqrt n\). Because the sample mean has a normal distribution with mean \(\mu\) and standard deviation \(\sigma/\sqrt n\), \[ \begin{aligned} \pi(\mu) &=P_\mu(\overline{X}>c)\\ &=1-\Phi\left(\frac{c-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n}\right)\\ &=1-\Phi\left(z_{1-\alpha} -\frac{\mu-\mu_0}{\sigma/\sqrt n}\right). \end{aligned} \]

For the example above, if the true mean is \(\mu=105\), then \[ \begin{aligned} \beta(105) &=P_{105}(\overline{X}\leq103.2897)\\ &=\Phi\left(\frac{103.2897-105}{2}\right) \approx0.19624,\\ \pi(105)&=1-\beta(105)\approx0.80376. \end{aligned} \] In repeated studies of this design, approximately \(80.4\%\) would reject \(H_0\) if the true mean were 105, and approximately \(19.6\%\) would fail to reject it. These are probabilities for repeated decisions, not probabilities that any particular conclusion is correct.

True mean \(\mu\) \(\pi(\mu)\) Interpretation

\(100\) \(0.0500\) Test size at the null boundary
\(102\) \(0.2595\) Low power for a small increase
\(105\) \(0.8038\) About 80% power
\(110\) \(0.9996\) Very high power for a large increase

Planning sample size before collecting data.

A power analysis starts with a prespecified effect worth detecting, an acceptable type I error level \(\alpha\), and a target power \(1-\beta\). For the right-sided, known-\(\sigma\) normal-mean test, suppose the true mean of interest is \(\mu_1=\mu_0+\delta\), with \(\delta>0\). Solving the power equation gives \[ n\geq\left( \frac{\sigma\left[z_{1-\alpha}+z_{1-\beta}\right]} {\delta} \right)^2. \]

For \(\sigma=10\), \(\delta=5\), \(\alpha=0.05\), and target power \(0.80\), \[ n\geq\left(\frac{10(1.64485+0.84162)}{5}\right)^2 \approx24.73. \] Therefore, we need at least \(n=25\) independent observations under this model. We must round the calculated sample size upward.

This formula applies specifically to a one-sided z-test with known standard deviation. A two-sided alternative, unknown variance, clustering, missing data, or other model features can change the required sample size. In practice, the target \(\delta\) should be chosen for its substantive importance rather than selected because it produces a convenient sample size.

Sample-size calculations are planning tools, not guarantees that an individual study will reach statistical significance. Their accuracy depends on the assumed variability, effect size, analysis procedure, and study design.

Statistical significance versus practical importance.

A result is called statistically significant at level \(\alpha\) when the chosen test rejects \(H_0\). This does not imply that the detected difference is large or important in practice. With sufficiently large samples, even a tiny departure from a null value may be detected reliably.

For example, suppose \(\sigma=1\) is known, \(n=10{,}000\), and an observed sample mean is \(\bar{x}=0.05\). For the two-sided test \(H_0:\mu=0\), the z-statistic is \[ z=\frac{0.05}{1/\sqrt{10{,}000}}=5, \] with two-sided p-value approximately \(5.73\times10^{-7}\). The result is statistically significant, but the difference is only \(0.05\) population standard deviations. Whether that is important depends on the application.

A corresponding 95% confidence interval is \[ 0.05\pm1.96\left(\frac{1}{100}\right) \approx(0.0304,0.0696). \] Reporting an effect estimate together with its uncertainty is usually more informative than reporting only a binary significance decision. We will define p-values and develop confidence intervals in later sections.

Why a 5% level does not mean 5% of discoveries are false.

Consider 1000 studies: suppose 900 null hypotheses are true and 100 alternatives are true. If each study uses a test of size \(0.05\), and power \(0.80\) at each true alternative, the expected numbers of rejections are \[ \begin{aligned} \text{False rejections: }&900(0.05)=45,\\ \text{Correct rejections: }&100(0.80)=80. \end{aligned} \] Thus, \(45/(45+80)=0.36\) is the ratio of these expected counts. This illustrative calculation shows why a 5% significance level does not imply that only 5% of reported significant results are false. The ratio of expectations is not, in general, the expected realized false-discovery proportion.

Multiple testing raises a related concern. If all 20 null hypotheses are true, 20 tests are independent, and each has size 0.05, the probability of at least one false rejection is \[ 1-(1-0.05)^{20}\approx0.6415. \] This probability is called the family-wise error rate (FWER). The formula requires independence and actual test size 0.05; it does not apply unchanged to arbitrary dependent tests.

A basic method for controlling the FWER is the Bonferroni correction: for \(m\) prespecified tests, test each hypothesis at level \(\alpha/m\). By the union bound, the probability of one or more false rejections is then at most \(\alpha\), without requiring independence. This protection can come at the cost of lower power.

Points to check before interpreting a test.

  • Non-rejection is not proof: failing to reject a null hypothesis does not establish that it is true. A study may have insufficient power to detect a meaningful effect.
  • Power requires a specified alternative: it depends on the effect size, sample size, variability, model, and test procedure.
  • Significance is not a posterior probability: neither a significance level nor a p-value gives \(P(H_0\mid\text{observed data})\) by itself.
  • Model assumptions matter: dependence, selection bias, missing observations, or a wrong sampling distribution can invalidate nominal error calculations.

  • Significance is not causality: a statistical association, however significant, need not be caused by the variable studied.

Critical Value and the p-Value

Once a null hypothesis, an alternative hypothesis, and a test statistic have been specified, we need a rule for deciding whether the observed data provide sufficiently strong evidence against the null. Two equivalent ways to express the decision for a given test are the critical-value approach and the p-value approach. Both depend on the distribution of the test statistic under the null hypothesis.

The test statistic and its null distribution.

Let \(X=(X_1,\ldots,X_n)\) denote the random observations and let \(T=T(X)\) be a test statistic. Before observing the data, \(T\) is a random variable; after observing \(\mathbf{x}\), its value \(t_{\mathrm{obs}}=T(\mathbf{x})\) is a fixed number. A null distribution describes the sampling distribution of \(T\) under a distribution permitted by \(H_0\). For a composite null, this distribution may depend on unknown nuisance parameters, or the test may use a statistic with a null law that does not depend on them.

For example, suppose \(X_1,\ldots,X_n\) are independent and normally distributed with unknown mean \(\mu\) and known standard deviation \(\sigma>0\). Under \(H_0:\mu=\mu_0\), the statistic \[ Z=\frac{\overline{X}-\mu_0}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}} \] follows the standard normal distribution \(N(0,1)\). This exact statement concerns the standardized sample mean, not the individual observations.

Worked example. Let \(n=25\), \(\mu_0=50\), \(\sigma=10\), and suppose the observed sample mean is \(\bar{x}=54.2\). Then \[ z_{\mathrm{obs}}=\frac{54.2-50}{10/\sqrt{25}}=2.10. \] The sample mean is 2.10 standard errors above the hypothesized mean. Whether this leads to rejection depends on the alternative and the significance level, not just on the sign of the statistic.

Definition (critical value and rejection region).

A rejection region is the set of observed outcomes for which a test rejects \(H_0\). When the decision depends on a statistic \(T\), the rejection region can be specified using values of \(T\). A critical value is a boundary used to define that region. The boundaries are chosen so that the probability of rejection under \(H_0\) is no greater than the chosen significance level \(\alpha\).

Let \(\Phi\) denote the standard normal CDF and \(\Phi^{-1}\) its quantile function. Consider the z-statistic above and a significance level \(\alpha\in(0,1)\). The usual rejection regions are as follows.

Alternative Critical value(s) Reject when
Right-tailed: \(\mu>\mu_0\) \(c_R=\Phi^{-1}(1-\alpha)\) \(z_{\mathrm{obs}}\geq c_R\)
Left-tailed: \(\mu<\mu_0\) \(c_L=\Phi^{-1}(\alpha)\) \(z_{\mathrm{obs}}\leq c_L\)
Two-sided: \(\mu\neq\mu_0\) \(\pm\Phi^{-1}(1-\alpha/2)\) \(|z_{\mathrm{obs}}|\geq\Phi^{-1}(1-\alpha/2)\)

At \(\alpha=0.05\), the right-tail cutoff is approximately \(1.6449\), the left-tail cutoff is approximately \(-1.6449\), and the two-sided cutoffs are approximately \(-1.9600\) and \(1.9600\). Thus, for \(z_{\mathrm{obs}}=2.10\), we reject for the right-tailed and two-sided alternatives but fail to reject for the left-tailed alternative.

The equal-tail two-sided rule is appropriate for this symmetric normal statistic. There is no universal requirement that a two-sided rejection region be symmetric or allocate equal probabilities to both tails; the rejection rule must be defined by the particular statistical test.

What does a p-value measure?

A p-value measures how incompatible an observed test statistic is with the specified null model, using the notion of extremeness defined by the test. Smaller p-values indicate stronger evidence against the null in that testing procedure. A p-value is not a probability assigned to \(H_0\) itself.

Definition (p-value for a nested family of tests).

Suppose \(\{R_\alpha:0<\alpha<1\}\) is a family of rejection regions for a specified test, with \(R_{\alpha_1}\subseteq R_{\alpha_2}\) whenever \(\alpha_1\leq\alpha_2\). The p-value for the observed outcome \(\mathbf{x}\) is \[ p(\mathbf{x})=\inf\bigl(\{\alpha\in(0,1):\mathbf{x}\in R_\alpha\}\cup\{1\}\bigr), \] so the infimum equals 1 if rejection never occurs at a level below 1. Informally, it is the smallest significance level at which this family of tests rejects the observed outcome (with this convention).

For the usual one-sided tests with a simple continuous null distribution, this leads to a tail probability: the probability under \(H_0\) of a statistic at least as extreme as the observed one in the direction of \(H_1\). For two-sided or discrete tests, what counts as at least as extreme must be specified rather than assumed.

For our standard normal z-test, the conventional p-values are \[ \begin{aligned} p_{\mathrm{right}}&=1-\Phi(z_{\mathrm{obs}}),\\ p_{\mathrm{left}}&=\Phi(z_{\mathrm{obs}}),\\ p_{\mathrm{two}}&=2\bigl[1-\Phi(|z_{\mathrm{obs}}|)\bigr]. \end{aligned} \] The factor of 2 in the two-sided formula follows from symmetry of the standard normal distribution; it is not a general formula for every test.

Worked example. With \(z_{\mathrm{obs}}=2.10\), we obtain \[ \begin{aligned} p_{\mathrm{right}}&\approx0.01786,\\ p_{\mathrm{left}}&\approx0.98214,\\ p_{\mathrm{two}}&\approx0.03573. \end{aligned} \] The observed data are unchanged, but the p-values differ because the alternatives ask different questions. The direction of the test should therefore be selected before examining the data.

Theorem (equivalence of decisions).

For the conventional continuous normal z-tests described above, the critical-value approach rejects at level \(\alpha\) if and only if the corresponding p-value satisfies \(p\leq\alpha\).

Proof. In the right-tailed case, the inequality \(1-\Phi(z_{\mathrm{obs}})\leq\alpha\) is equivalent to \(z_{\mathrm{obs}}\geq\Phi^{-1}(1-\alpha)\), since \(\Phi\) is strictly increasing. The left-tailed case is analogous. For a two-sided test, \(2[1-\Phi(|z_{\mathrm{obs}}|)]\leq\alpha\) is equivalent to \(|z_{\mathrm{obs}}|\geq\Phi^{-1}(1-\alpha/2)\).

More generally, when a p-value is defined from the nested rejection regions of a test, the two approaches express the same rejection rule (with suitable treatment of boundary cases). The p-value is useful because it reports the result independently of a single chosen \(\alpha\): a two-sided p-value of approximately \(0.03573\) rejects at the 5% level but not at the 1% level. The evidence has not changed; only the decision threshold has.

Student's t, chi-square, and F-distributions.

Critical values and p-values are not restricted to normal tests. The relevant null distribution depends on the statistic and on the assumptions used to derive its sampling law. For example:

  • A Student's t-statistic may arise when a normal population mean is tested with unknown standard deviation.
  • A chi-square statistic may arise in a goodness-of-fit test or a test concerning a normal population variance.
  • An F-statistic may arise in an analysis of variance (ANOVA) or when comparing nested regression models.

The reference distribution alone does not determine whether the test is left-tailed, right-tailed, or two-sided. That depends on the hypotheses and on how the statistic is constructed.

Worked examples. For a two-sided t-test with 9 degrees of freedom and \(t_{\mathrm{obs}}=2.30\), the p-value is approximately \(0.04700\). For a right-tailed chi-square test with 4 degrees of freedom and observed statistic \(13.28\), the p-value is approximately \(0.00999\). For a right-tailed F-test with numerator and denominator degrees of freedom 5 and 10 and observed statistic \(3.50\), it is approximately \(0.04349\). Each example rejects at the 5% level for its stated test.

Discrete tests and conservative significance levels.

For a discrete statistic, the possible rejection probabilities occur in steps. A non-randomized test may therefore have actual size strictly smaller than the nominal level \(\alpha\). Recall the example of 10 independent fair coin tosses under \(H_0:p=0.5\), with \(X\sim\operatorname{Binomial}(10,0.5)\), against \(H_1:p>0.5\).

If we reject for \(X\geq8\), the type I error probability is \[ P_{0.5}(X\geq8)=\frac{56}{1024}\approx0.05469, \] which exceeds \(0.05\). If instead we reject for \(X\geq9\), the probability is \[ P_{0.5}(X\geq9)=\frac{11}{1024}\approx0.01074. \] Thus, the latter rule is a valid level-0.05 test, but its actual size is approximately 1.07%.

If 8 heads are observed, the right-tailed p-value is \(P_{0.5}(X\geq8)\approx0.05469\), so we fail to reject at the 5% level. With 9 observed heads, the p-value is \(P_{0.5}(X\geq9)\approx0.01074\), so we reject. Notice the importance of including equality: for a discrete variable, \(P(X\geq k)\) and \(P(X>k)\) differ.

A two-sided test requires an additional convention for what counts as more extreme. For 8 observed heads under the fair-coin null, symmetry gives \[ P_{0.5}(X\leq2)+P_{0.5}(X\geq8)=0.109375. \] For an asymmetric binomial null, doubling a one-sided tail is not generally the same as a two-sided exact p-value. For example, SciPy's binomtest two-sided procedure sums the null probabilities of outcomes no more probable than the observed one.

The relationship with confidence intervals.

A confidence set can be constructed by retaining parameter values that the corresponding tests do not reject. In particular, for a two-sided normal z-test with known \(\sigma\), the null value \(\mu_0\) is rejected at level \(\alpha\) precisely when it lies outside the matching \(100(1-\alpha)\%\) confidence interval \[ \bar{x}\pm\Phi^{-1}(1-\alpha/2)\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}. \] The word matching is essential: different confidence interval procedures need not agree with tests constructed using different assumptions or rejection rules.

Worked example. Suppose \(\bar{x}=54.5\), \(n=25\), \(\sigma=10\), and \(H_0:\mu=50\) is tested against \(H_1:\mu\neq50\). The observed statistic is \(z=2.25\), giving the two-sided p-value \(p\approx0.02445\). The corresponding 95% confidence interval is \[ 54.5\pm1.9600\cdot2\approx(50.58,58.42). \] Because the interval excludes 50, both methods reject at the 5% level. The interval also reports a range of parameter values compatible with the observed data under this model.

How are p-values distributed under the null hypothesis?

Before the data are collected, the p-value is itself a random variable. For an exactly calibrated test with a continuous null distribution, the p-value is uniformly distributed on \([0,1]\) under the simple null hypothesis.

Theorem (uniformity of the two-sided normal p-value).

Let \(Z\sim N(0,1)\) and define \(P=2[1-\Phi(|Z|)]\). Then \[ P\sim\operatorname{Uniform}(0,1). \]

Proof. For \(0<u<1\), symmetry of the standard normal distribution gives \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{P}(P\leq u) &=\mathbb{P}\!\left(|Z|\geq\Phi^{-1}(1-u/2)\right)\\ &=2\left[1-\Phi\!\left(\Phi^{-1}(1-u/2)\right)\right]\\ &=u. \end{aligned} \] Hence the CDF of \(P\) is the uniform CDF on \([0,1]\).

Consequently, under a true null and the stated test assumptions, approximately 5% of independent repetitions produce p-values at most 0.05. This statement does not mean that a particular observed p-value of 0.05 assigns 5% probability to the null hypothesis being true or false.

For a valid conservative or discrete test, the appropriate general property is often super-uniformity: \[ \mathbb{P}_{\theta}(P\leq u)\leq u \qquad\text{for all }\theta\in\Theta_0,\;0\leq u\leq1. \] The inequality may be strict because the p-value cannot assume every possible number between 0 and 1.

Common interpretation errors.

  • A p-value is not \(\mathbb{P}(H_0\text{ is true}\mid\text{data})\). It is calculated from a specified null model. Hypothesis probabilities require additional modeling assumptions.
  • A large p-value does not establish that the null is true. It may reflect an effect too small to detect with the available sample, or a test with low power.
  • A small p-value does not measure effect size. Report an estimate and an uncertainty measure where possible, and assess whether the effect matters in practice.
  • The p-value depends on the entire testing procedure. The null model, alternative, statistic, and definition of extremeness all matter.
  • Selective testing and repeated analyses affect calibration. If many tests are performed and only the smallest p-value is reported without adjustment, the nominal single-test error interpretation can be misleading.

Inference for One Population Mean with Known Sigma

A one-sample z-test for a population mean examines whether the mean \(\mu\) of a population agrees with a specified value \(\mu_0\), when the population standard deviation \(\sigma>0\) is known independently of the sample being tested. Although this situation is less common than one in which \(\sigma\) must be estimated, it can arise in a stable measurement or manufacturing process with a well-established population variability. The normal-mean model also provides a useful starting point for understanding many other hypothesis tests.

Statistical model and notation.

Let \(X_1,\ldots,X_n\) be independent and identically distributed observations with \(\mathbb{E}(X_i)=\mu\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(X_i)=\sigma^2\), where \(\sigma\) is known. We observe a realization \(x_1,\ldots,x_n\) and calculate the sample mean \(\bar{x}=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^n x_i\). The random sample mean is \(\bar X=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i\). Independence implies \[ \mathbb{E}(\bar X)=\mu,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(\bar X) =\frac{1}{n^2}\sum_{i=1}^n\operatorname{Var}(X_i) =\frac{\sigma^2}{n}. \] Thus the standard error of \(\bar X\) is \(\sigma/\sqrt{n}\). It measures how much the sample mean varies across repeated independent samples of size \(n\); it is not the same as the standard deviation \(\sigma\) of individual observations.

If, more specifically, \(X_i\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)\), then sums of independent normal random variables are normal. Consequently, \[ \bar X\sim N\!\left(\mu,\frac{\sigma^2}{n}\right), \qquad \frac{\bar X-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}}\sim N(0,1). \] The second statement follows by subtracting the mean and dividing by the standard deviation. Under a null hypothesis \(H_0:\mu=\mu_0\), the z-test statistic therefore has the form \[ Z=\frac{\bar X-\mu_0}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}} \;\overset{H_0}{\sim}\;N(0,1). \] For observed data we write \(z_{\mathrm{obs}}=(\bar x-\mu_0)/(\sigma/\sqrt n)\). A value of \(z_{\mathrm{obs}}=2\), for example, means that the observed sample mean lies two standard errors above the hypothesized mean, not that it lies two population standard deviations above it.

Why normality matters. In the normal population model the result is exact for every \(n\geq 1\). If the observations are independent and identically distributed but not normal, and have finite positive variance, the central limit theorem gives \[ \frac{\sqrt n\,(\bar X-\mu)}{\sigma} \xrightarrow{\,d\,}N(0,1) \quad\text{as }n\to\infty. \] In that case the z-test is only approximately calibrated for sufficiently large samples. There is no universal sample-size threshold: skewness, heavy tails, rare outliers and dependence can make the approximation poor. In particular, the usual central limit theorem does not apply to a Cauchy population, which has no finite mean or variance.

Hypotheses, critical values and p-values.

The null and alternative hypotheses must be specified before inspecting the result. Let \(\Phi\) denote the cumulative distribution function of \(N(0,1)\), and \(z_q=\Phi^{-1}(q)\) its \(q\)-quantile. At significance level \(\alpha\), the standard z-tests are:

Alternative Rejection region p-value
Two-sided: \(H_1:\mu\ne\mu_0\) \(|z_{\mathrm{obs}}|\geq z_{1-\alpha/2}\) \(2[1-\Phi(|z_{\mathrm{obs}}|)]\)
Left-tailed: \(H_1:\mu<\mu_0\) \(z_{\mathrm{obs}}\leq z_\alpha\) \(\Phi(z_{\mathrm{obs}})\)
Right-tailed: \(H_1:\mu>\mu_0\) \(z_{\mathrm{obs}}\geq z_{1-\alpha}\) \(1-\Phi(z_{\mathrm{obs}})\)

The direction of a test changes its interpretation and its p-value. In a directional test it is mathematically more precise to formulate the null as \(H_0:\mu\leq\mu_0\) against \(H_1:\mu>\mu_0\), or \(H_0:\mu\geq\mu_0\) against \(H_1:\mu<\mu_0\). The boundary \(\mu=\mu_0\) is where the Type I error probability is largest, and it is this boundary distribution that is used to compute the critical value and p-value. In introductory calculations, the shorthand \(H_0:\mu=\mu_0\) is also common.

Worked numerical example: understanding the three alternatives. Suppose \(z_{\mathrm{obs}}=2.50\). The two-sided p-value is \(2[1-\Phi(2.50)]\approx0.01242\), the right-tailed p-value is \(1-\Phi(2.50)\approx0.00621\), and the left-tailed p-value is \(\Phi(2.50)\approx0.99379\). The same sample can support a claim that the mean is higher than \(\mu_0\) but provide no support for a claim that it is lower. The alternative must not be chosen after seeing which direction produces a smaller p-value.

A complete z-test: the weight of manufactured packages.

Suppose a filling machine produces packages whose weights can reasonably be modeled as independent normal observations with a process standard deviation of \(\sigma=12\) grams, established from separate calibration data. We want to investigate whether the current mean is different from its target \(\mu_0=500\) grams. A random sample of \(n=36\) packages has observed mean \(\bar x=505\) grams. These numbers constitute a hypothetical example; we are not claiming to have observed a particular factory.

Step 1 — Formulate the hypotheses. Since deviations on either side of the target are important, we choose a two-sided test: \(H_0:\mu=500\) versus \(H_1:\mu\ne500\). Set \(\alpha=0.05\) before looking at the sample.

Step 2 — Calculate the standard error and statistic. \[ \operatorname{SE}(\bar X)=\frac{12}{\sqrt{36}}=2\ \text{g}, \qquad z_{\mathrm{obs}}=\frac{505-500}{2}=2.50. \] Under \(H_0\), an observed mean this far from 500 grams is unusual relative to the standard error of 2 grams.

Step 3 — Critical-value approach. The critical values at the 5% level are \(\pm z_{0.975}\approx\pm1.960\). Equivalently, we reject whenever the observed mean falls outside \[ \left[500-1.960\cdot2,\;500+1.960\cdot2\right] \approx[496.08,503.92]\ \text{g}. \] Since \(505>503.92\), we reject \(H_0\).

Step 4 — p-value approach. By symmetry of the normal distribution, \[ p=\mathbb{P}_{H_0}(|Z|\geq2.50) =2\,[1-\Phi(2.50)] \approx0.01242. \] Since \(p<0.05\), the p-value approach gives the same decision. The data provide evidence that the current process mean differs from 500 grams, assuming the stated sampling model and independently known process variation. The test does not say that the probability that \(H_0\) is true is 1.242%.

Step 5 — Quantify the size of the departure. The estimated mean difference is \(\bar x-\mu_0=5\) grams. A two-sided 95% confidence interval for \(\mu\) is \[ \bar x\pm z_{0.975}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n} =505\pm1.960\cdot2 \approx[501.08,508.92]\ \text{g}. \] Since 500 grams is outside this interval, the corresponding two-sided 5% hypothesis test rejects \(H_0\). Whether a mean departure of roughly five grams is important for production depends on engineering tolerances and costs; a small p-value does not settle that question.

What if the question had been one-sided? Had we specified \(H_1:\mu>500\) in advance, the right-tailed critical value would be \(z_{0.95}\approx1.645\) and the p-value would be \(1-\Phi(2.50)\approx0.00621\). But this test would not establish that the mean differs in either direction. A lower mean might also matter in practice, which is a good reason to use the two-sided formulation here.

Python: calculating the test from summary statistics.

Since the z-test uses only \(\bar x\), \(\mu_0\), \(\sigma\) and \(n\), we do not need the individual observations to perform this particular calculation. SciPy supplies the normal CDF (norm.cdf), upper-tail survival function (norm.sf) and quantile function (norm.ppf). sf is preferable to subtracting cdf from 1 when the tail probability is very small. 

import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import norm

mu_0 = 500.0
sigma = 12.0           # Assumed known from independent calibration
n = 36
x_bar = 505.0
alpha = 0.05

se = sigma / np.sqrt(n)
z = (x_bar - mu_0) / se

p_two_sided = 2 * norm.sf(abs(z))
p_right = norm.sf(z)
p_left = norm.cdf(z)

critical_z = norm.ppf(1 - alpha / 2)
critical_low = mu_0 - critical_z * se
critical_high = mu_0 + critical_z * se
confidence_interval = (
    x_bar - critical_z * se,
    x_bar + critical_z * se,
)

print(f"Standard error: {se:.3f}")
print(f"z-statistic: {z:.3f}")
print(f"Two-sided p-value: {p_two_sided:.6f}")
print(f"Left-tailed p-value: {p_left:.6f}")
print(f"Right-tailed p-value: {p_right:.6f}")
print(f"Rejection if x_bar < {critical_low:.3f} or x_bar > {critical_high:.3f}")
print(f"95% CI: ({confidence_interval[0]:.3f}, {confidence_interval[1]:.3f})")
print("Reject H0:", p_two_sided <= alpha)

The output is \(\operatorname{SE}=2.000\), \(z=2.500\), two-sided p-value \(0.012419\), right-tailed p-value \(0.006210\), left-tailed p-value \(0.993790\), and 95% confidence interval \((501.080,508.920)\) grams (rounded to three decimal places). We reject the two-sided null at the 5% level.

A second example: a left-tailed test.

Suppose \(X_i\sim N(\mu,8^2)\) independently, and we want to test \(H_0:\mu\geq40\) against \(H_1:\mu<40\). A sample of \(n=64\) has \(\bar x=38\). Here \[ z_{\mathrm{obs}}=\frac{38-40}{8/\sqrt{64}}=-2, \qquad p=\Phi(-2)\approx0.02275. \] Since \(-2<-1.645\), we reject at \(\alpha=0.05\), providing evidence for a mean below 40. If the alternative had instead been two-sided, the p-value would be \(2\Phi(-2)\approx0.04550\), also significant at 5% but not at 1%. Note that the sign of the statistic matters for a one-sided test.

A third example: a small sample with an exactly normal population.

Consider four independent observations \(11,12,13,12\) from a normal population with known \(\sigma=2\). We test \(H_0:\mu=10\) against \(H_1:\mu\ne10\). Although \(n=4\) is small, a normal-population z-test is exact: \[ \bar x=\frac{11+12+13+12}{4}=12, \qquad z_{\mathrm{obs}}=\frac{12-10}{2/\sqrt4}=2, \qquad p=2[1-\Phi(2)]\approx0.04550. \] We reject at 5%. A 95% confidence interval is \(12\pm1.960\cdot1\approx[10.04,13.96]\), which narrowly excludes 10. The conclusion relies heavily on the assumed normal population and known \(\sigma\); a sample of four observations cannot establish either assumption. 

import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import norm

observations = np.array([11.0, 12.0, 13.0, 12.0])
mu_0 = 10.0
sigma = 2.0
n = len(observations)

z = (observations.mean() - mu_0) / (sigma / np.sqrt(n))
p_value = 2 * norm.sf(abs(z))
print(z, p_value)   # 2.0, approximately 0.0455

Assumptions and diagnostic questions.

  • Known \(\sigma\). The standard deviation used in the denominator must be justified by information external to the current sample; using its sample standard deviation instead changes the statistical procedure. A historically calibrated value is usable only if it remains credible for the process now being studied.
  • Independent sampling. Each observation should contribute genuinely independent information. Repeated measurements on one individual, clustered observations and serially correlated time series do not generally satisfy this assumption. Sampling without replacement from a finite population also introduces dependence; a finite-population correction or a design-based analysis may be needed when the sampling fraction is not negligible.
  • Normality or a justified approximation. Exact normal-test calculations require a normal sampling distribution of \(\bar X\), which follows from independent normal observations. If this is not plausible, investigate the data distribution, outliers and whether a CLT approximation is justified. A Q-Q plot and histogram can help diagnose shape, but cannot prove normality, especially for small samples.
  • Meaningful target population. Good mathematics does not repair a biased sampling design. A convenient sample of students cannot automatically support conclusions about all European adults or all future students. Missing data, selection effects and changing data-generating processes must also be considered.

Counterexample: using the wrong population standard deviation. Suppose the observations are actually normal with \(\sigma=12\), but we mistakenly conduct a two-sided 5% z-test using \(\sigma_{\mathrm{used}}=8\). Under \(H_0\), the statistic then has standard deviation \(12/8=1.5\), rather than 1. The actual Type I error probability is \[ \mathbb{P}_{H_0}(|Z_{\mathrm{used}}|\geq1.960) =2\left[1-\Phi\!\left(1.960\frac{8}{12}\right)\right] \approx0.1913. \] In other words, a procedure advertised as a 5% test rejects a true null about 19.1% of the time under this misspecified model. Knowledge of \(\sigma\) is not a harmless technicality.

Simulation: checking the nominal false-positive rate.

We can verify the Type I error probability by simulating many datasets under \(H_0\). The simulation uses the correct known \(\sigma=12\) and compares it with the misspecified choice of 8. Since the data are independent and normal, the theoretical false-positive probabilities are 5% and approximately 19.1%. 

import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import norm

rng = np.random.default_rng(2026)
repetitions = 50_000
n = 36
mu_0 = 500.0
sigma_true = 12.0
alpha = 0.05

# Under H0, sample means are exactly normal.
means = rng.normal(mu_0, sigma_true / np.sqrt(n), size=repetitions)
cutoff = norm.ppf(1 - alpha / 2)

z_correct = (means - mu_0) / (sigma_true / np.sqrt(n))
z_wrong = (means - mu_0) / (8.0 / np.sqrt(n))

print("Correct sigma:", np.mean(np.abs(z_correct) >= cutoff))
print("Wrong sigma:", np.mean(np.abs(z_wrong) >= cutoff))
print("Theoretical wrong-sigma rate:",
      2 * norm.sf(cutoff * 8.0 / sigma_true))

The first simulation proportion should be close to \(0.05\), and the second close to \(0.1913\). Small differences across runs are ordinary Monte Carlo sampling error; setting the random seed makes the printed results reproducible.

Power and sample-size planning for a z-test.

Under a true mean \(\mu\), the z-test statistic is not centered at zero; rather, \[ Z\sim N\!\left(\delta,1\right),\qquad \delta=\frac{\sqrt n(\mu-\mu_0)}{\sigma}. \] The quantity \(\delta\) is the noncentrality parameter: a true departure of the mean from \(\mu_0\), measured in standard errors. It increases in magnitude when the sample size increases, the true mean moves farther from \(\mu_0\), or the population becomes less variable.

For a two-sided test with critical value \(c=z_{1-\alpha/2}\), its power function is \[ \pi(\mu)=\mathbb{P}_{\mu}(|Z|\geq c) =\Phi(-c-\delta)+1-\Phi(c-\delta). \] To derive this, split the rejection event into its disjoint lower and upper tails: \(\mathbb{P}_{\mu}(Z\leq-c)+\mathbb{P}_{\mu}(Z\geq c)\), and standardize using \(Z-\delta\sim N(0,1)\). When \(\mu=\mu_0\), we have \(\delta=0\) and therefore \(\pi(\mu_0)=\alpha\), as expected.

Worked power example. In the package-weight problem, suppose the true mean is 505 grams while \(\mu_0=500\), \(\sigma=12\), \(n=36\), and \(\alpha=0.05\). Then \(\delta=2.50\), so \[ \pi(505)=\Phi(-1.960-2.50)+1-\Phi(1.960-2.50) \approx0.7054. \] Repeating this experiment many times, the two-sided test would detect this five-gram shift about 70.5% of the time. That is the probability of rejecting the null when the assumed alternative is true; it is not the probability that the alternative is true given this particular dataset.

A useful approximate sample-size formula for a two-sided z-test aiming for power \(1-\beta\) against a meaningful shift \(\Delta=|\mu_1-\mu_0|>0\) is \[ n\approx \left(\frac{\sigma\left(z_{1-\alpha/2}+z_{1-\beta}\right)}{\Delta}\right)^2. \] For \(\sigma=12\), \(\Delta=5\), \(\alpha=0.05\) and 80% power, this gives \(n\approx45.21\), suggesting \(n=46\) after rounding up. The exact two-sided normal-model power at \(n=46\) is approximately \(0.8068\). Because the formula neglects the typically tiny opposite-tail rejection probability, always verify the resulting integer sample size using the exact power function. 

import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import norm

mu_0 = 500.0
mu_true = 505.0
sigma = 12.0
alpha = 0.05
c = norm.ppf(1 - alpha / 2)

def two_sided_power(n, mu_true, mu_0, sigma, alpha=0.05):
    c = norm.ppf(1 - alpha / 2)
    delta = np.sqrt(n) * (mu_true - mu_0) / sigma
    return norm.cdf(-c - delta) + norm.sf(c - delta)

for n in (9, 36, 46, 100):
    print(n, f"{two_sided_power(n, mu_true, mu_0, sigma, alpha):.4f}")

# Smallest integer n with at least 80% exact power.
required_n = next(
    n for n in range(2, 1000)
    if two_sided_power(n, mu_true, mu_0, sigma, alpha) >= 0.80
)
print("Required sample size:", required_n)

The power values for \(n=9,36,46,100\) are approximately \(0.2395,0.7054,0.8068,0.9863\), respectively; the smallest integer sample size reaching at least 80% power is 46 under this model. This calculation assumes that the independent-observation, known-\(\sigma\), normal-model conditions will be credible in the planned study.

Inference for One Population Mean with Unknown Sigma

A hypothesis test for a population mean, when the population standard deviation \(\sigma\) is unknown, is conducted in the same way as if the population standard deviation were known. However, we need to consider the sample standard deviation \(s\) in addition, and the test statistic, denoted by \(t\), is defined as follows: \[t = \frac{\bar{x} - \mu_0}{s / \sqrt{n}}\text{.}\]

This test statistic is called the Student's \(t\)-distribution with \(n-1\) degrees of freedom.

We will again consider the students data set. We will examine the average weight of a random sample of students and compare it to the average weight of European adults. We will not showcase the critical value approach. For the p-value approach, we will use the stats.ttest_1samp() function from the scipy package.

We set the population mean accordingly: \(μ_0=70.8\). Further, we take a random sample (sample_weights) with a sample size of \(n=9\). The sample consists of the weights in kg of 9 randomly picked students from the students data set.

>>> n = 9
>>> sample_weights = students.sample(n, random_state = 9)["weight"]

The following code performs a one-sample t-test. The test is two-sided, and the result includes the t-statistic, p-value, and degrees of freedom.

>>> test_result = stats.ttest_1samp(sample_weights,
...                                 mu_0,
...                                 alternative = "two-sided")

Inference for Two Independent Population Means with Equal Standard Deviations

So far, we have focused on hypothesis tests for one population mean. However, in many applications, we want to compare the means of two or more populations. Therefore, we first need to distinguish between samples from two independent populations and samples from populations that are not independent, which are called paired samples.

We assume that the standard deviations of the two populations are equal but unknown. If, however, the population standard deviation \(\sigma\) were known, the test statistic could be written as follows: \[ z = \frac{(\bar{x}_{1} - \bar{x}_{2}) - (\mu_{1} - \mu_{2})} {\sigma \sqrt{\frac{1}{n_{1}} + \frac{1}{n_{2}}}} \]

In most real-world applications, \(\sigma\) is unknown and must therefore be estimated. A natural approach is to use the sample variances \(s_1^2\) and \(s_2^2\) as estimates of \(\sigma^2\). By pooling these variances and weighting them according to their respective sample sizes, we obtain the following estimate of \(\sigma\): \[ s_{p} = \sqrt{\frac{(n_{1} - 1)s_{1}^{2} + (n_{2} - 1)s_{2}^{2}}{n_{1} + n_{2} - 2}}. \]

The quantity \(s_p\) is called the pooled sample standard deviation, where the subscript \(p\) denotes pooling.

Replacing \(\sigma\) in the test statistic with its estimate \(s_p\) yields: \[ t = \frac{(\bar{x}_{1} - \bar{x}_{2}) - (\mu_{1} - \mu_{2})}{s_{p} \sqrt{\frac{1}{n_{1}} + \frac{1}{n_{2}}}} \]

The denominator of this expression, \(s_p \sqrt{\frac{1}{n_{1}} + \frac{1}{n_{2}}}\), is an estimator of the standard deviation of \(\bar{x}_{1} - \bar{x}_{2}\), which can be written as \(s_{\bar{x}_{1} - \bar{x}_{2}}\).

Note that the test statistic \(t\) follows a t-distribution with degrees of freedom given by: \[ \text{df} = n_{1} + n_{2} - 2. \]

A \(100(1 - \alpha)\%\) confidence interval for \(\mu_{1} - \mu_{2}\) is given by: \[ (\bar{x}_{1} - \bar{x}_{2}) \pm t \cdot s_{p} \sqrt{\frac{1}{n_{1}} + \frac{1}{n_{2}}} \]

Here, the value of \(t\) is obtained from the t-distribution corresponding to the desired confidence level and \(n_{1} + n_{2} - 2\) degrees of freedom.

Python allows us to conduct a pooled t-test using the ttest_ind_from_stats() over the stats module from the scipy package.

Inference for Two Independent Population Means with Unequal Standard Deviations

In cases where we want to test for two population means and the standard deviations are different between the two populations, the so-called non-pooled t-test, or Welch’s t-test, is applied.

The non-pooled t-test is very similar to the pooled t-test, except for the test statistic \(t\) and the calculation of the degrees of freedom (\(df\)). The test statistic does not use \(s_p\), the pooled standard deviation, and is written as: \[ t = \frac{\bar{x}_{1} - \bar{x}_{2}} {\sqrt{\frac{s^{2}_{1}}{n_{1}} + \frac{s^{2}_{2}}{n_{2}} }} \]

The denominator of the equation above is the estimator of the standard deviation of \(\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2\), denoted \(s_{\bar{x}_1-\bar{x}_2}\).

The test statistic \(t\) has a t-distribution, and the degrees of freedom (df) are given by \[ df=\frac{\left(\frac{s_1^2}{n_1}+\frac{s_2^2}{n_2}\right)^2} {\frac{\left(\frac{s_1^2}{n_1}\right)^2}{n_1-1} +\frac{\left(\frac{s_2^2}{n_2}\right)^2}{n_2-1}} \text{.} \]

The non-pooled t-test is robust to moderate violations of the normal population assumption, but it is less robust to outliers (Weiss, 2010).

The \(100(1-\alpha)\%\) confidence interval for \(\mu_1 - \mu_2\) is given by

\[ (\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2)\; \pm\; t^{\ast} \cdot \sqrt{\frac{s_1^{2}}{n_1} + \frac{s_2^{2}}{n_2}} \]

where the value of t is obtained from the t-distribution for the given confidence level. The degrees of freedom (df) and obtained using the equation above.

Inferences for Two Population Means Using Paired Samples

Let us now turn to a hypothesis-testing procedure for the difference between two population means when the samples are dependent. If, for example, two data values are collected from the same source (or element), these are called paired or matched samples.

These procedures are often applied in Before–After–Control–Impact (BACI) analysis. Imagine you are asked to evaluate the effectiveness of a filtering system in removing air pollutants released by a factory. One population consists of air-quality measurements taken before the filtering system is implemented or renewed, and the other population consists of measurements taken after installing the new filter system. In this case, you are dealing with paired samples because both data sets are collected from the same source, i.e., the factory.

In paired samples, the difference between the data values of the two samples is denoted by \(d\), often called the paired difference. Note that the sample size \(n\) for each sample is equal. The mean of the paired differences is denoted by \(\bar{d}\): \[ \bar{d} = \frac{\sum d}{n} \]

The standard deviation of the paired differences, \(s_d\), is calculated as: \[ s_d = \sqrt{\frac{\sum d^{2} - \frac{(\sum d)^{2}}{n}}{n - 1}} \]

Suppose that the paired-difference variable \(d\) is normally distributed. Then the paired t-statistic is expressed as: \[ t = \frac{\bar{d} - (\mu_{1} - \mu_{2})}{\frac{s_d}{\sqrt{n}}} \]

This simplifies to \(t = \frac{\bar{d}}{\frac{s_d}{\sqrt{n}}}\) when \(\mu_{1} - \mu_{2} = 0\). The test statistic \(t\) for paired samples follows a t-distribution with \(n - 1\) degrees of freedom.

The 100(1 − \(\alpha\))% confidence interval for \(\mu_d\) is: \[ \bar{d} \pm t \times \frac{s_d}{\sqrt{n}} \] where the value of \(t\) is obtained from the t-distribution for the given confidence level and \(n - 1\) degrees of freedom.

To demonstrate the paired t-test for dependent samples, we will investigate whether an online statistics learning tutorial helps students improve their grades.

There are two research questions of interest:

Inferences for One Population Standard Deviation

So far, we have discussed methods of inferential statistics that focus on making inferences about one or more population means. We will now consider methods of inferential statistics that focus on inferences for population variances (or standard deviations).

The test statistic for a hypothesis test concerning the population standard deviation, with the null hypothesis \(H_0: \sigma = \sigma_0\), for a normally distributed variable is calculated as: \[ \chi^{2} = \frac{(n - 1) s^{2}}{\sigma^{2}_{0}} \text{.} \]

This statistic follows a chi-square distribution, which is why it is denoted by the symbol \(\chi^2\), with \(n - 1\) degrees of freedom.

The \(100(1-\alpha)\%\) confidence interval for \(\sigma\) is given by: \[\sqrt { \frac {n - 1} {\chi^2_{\alpha / 2}}} \le \sigma \le \sqrt { \frac{n - 1} {\chi^2_{1 - \alpha / 2}} }\text{,}\]

where \(n\) is the sample size of the sample data.

It is important to note that the chi-square test for standard deviation is not robust to violations of the normality assumption. If the data is not normally distributed, the results of the test may be unreliable (Weiss, 2010).

In order to showcase the one standard deviation \(\chi^2\)-test, we examine the spread of the height in cm of female students and compare it to the spread of the height of all students (our population). We want to test whether the standard deviation of the height of female students is significantly smaller than the standard deviation of the height of all students.

The following code speaks for itself:

As we can see, the data falls roughly along a straight line. Based on this graphical evaluation, we conclude that the variable of interest is approximately normally distributed.

We now conduct the one–standard-deviation \(\chi^2\) test from scratch in Python. The null hypothesis is \(H_0: \sigma = \sigma_0\), and the alternative hypothesis is \(H_1: \sigma < \sigma_0\). We choose a significance level of \(\alpha=0.05\).

Inferences for Two Population Standard Deviations

In this section, we discuss hypothesis tests for two population standard deviations. In other words, we present methods of inference for the standard deviations of one variable from two different populations. These methods are based on the F-distribution, named in honour of Sir Ronald Aylmer Fisher.

The \(F\)-distribution is a right-skewed probability distribution with two shape parameters, \(v_1\) and \(v_2\), called the degrees of freedom for the numerator (\(v_1\)) and the denominator (\(v_2\)). \[df = (v_1, v_2)\]

To perform a hypothesis test for two population standard deviations, we calculate the value corresponding to a specified area under an \(F\)-curve.

The hypothesis-testing procedure for two standard deviations is called the two-standard-deviations \(F\)-test.

The test statistic for a hypothesis test involving a normally distributed variable and independent samples of sizes \(n_1\) and \(n_2\) is given by \[F = \frac {s_{1}^{2} / \sigma_{1}^{2}} {s_{2}^{2} / \sigma_{2}^{2}} \text{,}\]

with \(df = (n_1 - 1, n_2 - 1)\).

If \(H_0: \sigma_1 = \sigma_2\) is true, then the equation simplifies to \[F = \frac {s_{1}^{2}} {s_{2}^{2}} \text{.}\]

To illustrate the two-standard-deviations \(F\)-test, we once again examine the height variable in the students data set. We want to investigate whether the standard deviation of the height of female students (\(\sigma_1\)) is statistically different from the standard deviation of the height of male students (\(\sigma_2\)).

Chi-Square Goodness-of-Fit Test

The \(\chi^2\)-goodness-of-fit test is used to perform hypothesis tests on the distribution of a qualitative (categorical) variable or on a discrete quantitative variable that has only finitely many possible values.

The basic idea of the \(\chi^2\)-goodness-of-fit test is to compare frequencies. We compare a sample’s observed frequencies with the corresponding expected frequencies.

The Chi-Square Independence Test

The \(\chi^2\) independence test is an inferential method used to determine whether an association exists between two variables. As with other hypothesis tests, the null hypothesis states that the two variables are not associated, whereas the alternative hypothesis states that the two variables are associated.

Recall that statistically dependent variables are called associated variables, while variables that are not associated are called statistically independent variables. Also recall the concept of contingency tables (also known as two-way tables, cross-tabulation tables, or cross tabs).

The basic idea behind the \(\chi^2\) independence test is to compare the observed frequencies in a contingency table with the expected frequencies, assuming that the null hypothesis of non-association is true. The expected frequency for each cell of a contingency table is given by \[ E = \frac{R \times C}{n} \]

where \(R\) is the row total, \(C\) is the column total, and \(n\) is the sample size.

Once we know the expected frequencies, we must check two assumptions:

• All expected frequencies must be at least 1.
• At most 20 % of the expected frequencies may be less than 5.

We can confirm that both assumptions are satisfied by inspecting the table.

The comparison is based on the \(\chi^2\) test statistic for the observed and expected frequencies. The \(\chi^2\) test statistic follows the \(\chi^2\) distribution and is given by: \[ \chi^{2}= \sum \frac{(O - E)^{2}}{E} \]

where \(O\) represents the observed frequency and \(E\) represents the expected frequency. Note that \(\frac{(O - E)^2}{E}\) is calculated for each cell and then summed.

The number of degrees of freedom is given by: \[ df = (r - 1) \times (c - 1) \]

where \(r\) and \(c\) are the numbers of possible values for the two variables under consideration.

If the null hypothesis is true, the observed and expected frequencies are roughly equal, resulting in a small value of the \(\chi^2\) test statistic, which supports \(H_0\). However, if the value of the \(\chi^2\) test statistic is large, the data provide evidence against \(H_0\).

Inferences About the Slope

The linear model is given by the equation \[ y = \alpha + \beta x + \epsilon \]

where \(\alpha\) is the intercept, \(\beta\) is the regression coefficient, and \(\epsilon\) is the error term. The best regression line is found by applying the ordinary least squares method, which minimizes the sum of squared errors (SSE). This means minimizing the squared difference between the measured response variable \(y\) and the model prediction \(\hat{y}\), which is given by \[ \mathrm{SSE} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \epsilon_i^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2 \]

We will go into more detail when we discuss linear regression.

The regression t-test is applied to test whether the slope \(\beta\) of the population regression line is equal to \(0\). Based on this test, we may decide whether \(x\) is a useful (linear) predictor of \(y\).

The test statistic follows a t-distribution with \(df = n - 2\) degrees of freedom and can be written as \[ t = \frac{\beta}{s_b} = \frac{\beta}{\dfrac{s_e}{\sqrt{\sum (x - \bar{x})^2}}} \] where \(\beta\) corresponds to the sample regression coefficient and \(s_e\) to the residual standard error (also known as the standard error of the estimate), given by \[ s_e = \sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{SSE}}{n - 2}} \text{.} \]

The \(100(1 - \alpha)\%\) confidence interval for \(\beta\) is given by \[ \beta \pm t_{\alpha/2} \times \frac{s_e}{\sqrt{\sum (x - \bar{x})^2}} \text{.} \]

The value of \(t_{\alpha/2}\) is obtained from the t-distribution for the given confidence level and \(n - 2\) degrees of freedom.

In order to illustrate the regression t-test, we examine the relationship between two variables: students’ height as the predictor variable and students’ weight as the response variable. The question is whether height is a useful predictor of students’ weight.

The null hypothesis states that there is no linear relationship between the height and the weight of the individuals in the students data set, \(H_0: \beta = 0\), meaning that the predictor variable is not useful for making predictions. The alternative hypothesis states that there is a linear relationship, \(H_A: \beta \neq 0\), meaning that the predictor variable is useful for making predictions.

In order to conduct a regression t-test in Python using the stats module from the scipy package, we apply the linregress() function. We only need to provide our data separately as NumPy arrays. Furthermore, the test method can be specified using the alternative argument. Because the default is already set to two-sided, no adjustment is required.

Inferences About the Linear Correlation Coefficient

In order to test whether two variables are linearly correlated, or in other words, whether there is a linear relationship between them, we may apply the so-called correlation t-test. The population linear correlation coefficient, \(\rho\), measures the linear correlation between two variables in the same manner as the sample linear correlation coefficient, \(r\), measures the linear correlation between two variables in a sample of paired observations. Both \(\rho\) and \(r\) describe the strength of the linear relationship between two variables; however, \(r\) is an estimate of \(\rho\) obtained from sample data.

The linear correlation coefficient of two variables lies between \(-1\) and \(1\). If \(\rho = 0\), the variables are linearly uncorrelated; thus, there is no linear relationship between them. If \(\rho \neq 0\), the variables are linearly correlated. If \(\rho > 0\), the variables are positively linearly correlated, whereas if \(\rho < 0\), they are negatively linearly correlated.

A commonly used statistic to quantify the linear relationship between quantitative variables is the Pearson product–moment correlation coefficient. It is given by \[ r = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})} {\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n} (x_i - \bar{x})^2}\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - \bar{y})^2}} = \frac{s_{xy}}{s_x s_y} \]

where \(s_{xy}\) is the covariance of \(x\) and \(y\), and \(s_x\) and \(s_y\) are the standard deviations of \(x\) and \(y\), respectively.

Since the sample linear correlation coefficient \(r\) is an estimate of the population linear correlation coefficient \(\rho\), we may use \(r\) to perform a hypothesis test for \(\rho\). The test statistic for a correlation test follows a t-distribution with \(n - 2\) degrees of freedom and can be written as \[ t = \frac{r\sqrt{n - 2}}{\sqrt{1 - r^2}}. \]

In order to showcase the correlation t-test, we examine the relationship between the variable score1 and score2, which show the result of two mandatory statistics exams. The question is whether a linear relationship exists between the grades of two consecutive statistics exams.

The null hypothesis states that there is no linear relationship between the grades of two consecutive statistics exams, \(H_0: r = 0\). Recall that the formulation of the alternative hypothesis dictates whether a two-sided, left-tailed, or right-tailed hypothesis test is applied. The alternative hypothesis \(H_A: r \neq 0\) results in a two-sided hypothesis test.

In order to conduct a correlation t-test in Python using the stats module from the scipy package, we apply the pearsonr() function. We only need to provide our data separately as NumPy arrays. Furthermore, the test method can be specified using the alternative argument. Because the default is already set to two-sided, no adjustment is required.

Linear Regression

Regression analysis is a statistical process for estimating the relationships between two or more variables. The relationship is modeled as \( y \sim x \) or \( y = f(x) \). Both model descriptions indicate that the variable \( y \) is a function of \( x \). Therefore the variable \( y \) is denoted as the response or dependent variable, whereas the variable \( x \) is denoted as the predictor or independent variable.
